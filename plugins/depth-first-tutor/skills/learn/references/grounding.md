# Grounding: a source before teaching

Teaching from memory introduces silent errors, and the learner is exactly the person who can't detect
them. The risk is highest in exam preparation, where the exam follows canonical material and the gap
between the right wording and the almost-right one is what separates the correct option from a
distractor.

## Before you explain a new topic

Find and read a reliable source first. If the topic is covered by a source already in the tree's
`Sources:`, reuse it instead of searching again.

## How much to read

Ground the topic, not every sentence. At the start, pick one or two reliable sources that cover the
whole goal (an official document, a textbook chapter, the standard) and read the parts you need.
Reuse them for every node they cover. Look something up again only when a node goes beyond them, or
to check a precise claim: a number, a version, a formula, a definition, or wording an exam will test.
Don't fetch again what you already read in this session, and keep it to one or two lookups per turn
unless the learner asks to go deeper. In exam preparation, or when the learner brought their own
material, check every claim the exam could test against that material.

**Counts as a source:** official documentation, an established textbook in the field, a
standard or law in its official text, a peer-reviewed paper, official exam or course material.

**Doesn't count:** a blog with no author, a cram-course summary, AI-generated text, your own memory.

## When the learner brings material

Slides from their course, the exam syllabus, the book the exam board uses: that material defines what
"correct" means for their goal, so it comes first. Check it against a canonical source, and when the
two disagree, say so and show both instead of choosing silently. A cram summary the learner brings
still doesn't count as grounding: it is something to check, not something to check against.

## Recording

- Add every source to the tree's `Sources:` list, as a full reference or link, then `·` and what it
  supports. A source added mid-session goes there too.
- Keep claims traceable to a source. If a claim has none, say that it comes from you and is
  unverified.

## Checking silently

Check your premises against the source in your own reasoning. Don't narrate the check ("I looked it up
and..."). Speak up only when the source contradicts what you were about to say or doesn't settle the
question.

## No access

If you can't reach a source (no web access, a paywall, no file), say so before you start and ask the
learner for one. If the session goes on without a source, mark the tree `Sources: ⚠️ ungrounded`, so a
future session knows the content was not verified. Never teach from memory while implying a source.
